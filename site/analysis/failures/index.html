Models get the quiet responses right and miss the strong ones

Scored with a fixed rule that credits a correct “almost nothing happens”, the hard items are clear, well-measured cytokine responses that models predict only partly.

1 · How a model is scored

Each item is one cytokine in one donor's cell type. Which test applies depends on how clearly the response stands above noise.

How each quantity is computed

Response, ΔMean expression of treated cells minus untreated (PBS) cells, same donor and cell type, per gene (log counts per 10,000, 2,000 most variable genes).Δ = mean(treated) − mean(control)
NoiseHow large Δ would be if the cytokine did nothing, from the spread of untreated cells and the cell counts.√( Σgenes σ²control · (1/ntreated + 1/ncontrol) )
Signal-to-noiseSize of the response over the noise. Near 1: indistinguishable from no effect.‖Δ‖ ÷ noise
Explained R²Share of the measured response the prediction accounts for.1 − ‖prediction − Δ‖² ÷ ‖Δ‖²
Achievable R²What a model that knew the true response would reach: the rest of Δ is noise.1 − (noise ÷ ‖Δ‖)²
Error ratioDistance from the measured response in units of noise. A perfect model lands near 1.‖prediction − Δ‖ ÷ noise
Donor agreementCorrelation of this donor's Δ with the same cytokine and cell type in the other donors.corr(Δdonor, mean Δother donors)
Receptor expressionExpression in untreated cells of the weakest subunit of the best receptor complex.maxcomplexes minsubunits mean(control)

What this means

For model builders

Getting the direction right is not enough on strong responses: the size must match too. And do not invent responses where the cell barely reacts.

For biologists

The open problems are the strong, well-measured responses: common gamma chain cytokines and interferons, above all in monocytes and CD4 T cells.

For the benchmark

A fixed rule keeps quiet and null responses scorable and stops crediting a model for copying the average response.
